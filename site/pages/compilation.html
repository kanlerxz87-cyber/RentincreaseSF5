The question you asked, answered first: Do I, as an individual property owner (not a corporation), still need to inform the Rent Board and apply for a license before notifying tenants of a rent increase?

Yes. Under SF Administrative Code Sec. 37.15 (added by Ordinance No. 265-20, effective January 18, 2021), every owner of a residential dwelling unit in San Francisco — individual or corporate — must (1) report the unit into the Rent Board Housing Inventory and (2) obtain a rent increase license before imposing any annual and/or banked rent increase on a tenant. The license is automatically issued once the Housing Inventory report is submitted. A rent increase notice is not enforceable unless a current rent increase license is on file with the Rent Board on the effective date of the notice. For increases that require a Rent Board petition (capital improvements, operating expenses, etc.), the petition must be filed before tenants are served with any notice of increase. There is no exemption for individual owners. Sources: SF Admin Code Sec. 37.15 · Ordinance 265-20 announcement · Housing Inventory.

1. Executive summary

This report documents the process for raising rents on two rent-controlled units in San Francisco's Outer Sunset (ZIP 94122), a building built in 1940. It answers the owner's core question — whether an individual owner must inform the Rent Board and obtain a license before notifying tenants — and sets out what is a reasonable, normal, and lawful rent increase under local, state, and federal law. All findings are verified against official sources; every table row links to the source.

Key findings

a) A "rent increase license" is required — including for individual owners. San Francisco's Housing Inventory law (SF Admin Code Sec. 37.15) requires every owner of a residential unit to report unit information to the Rent Board annually (by March 1). Once a unit is reported as tenant-occupied, the Rent Board automatically issues a rent increase license. A landlord may not impose any annual or banked rent increase unless a current license is on file on the effective date of the increase; the tenant is not obligated to pay increases during any period of license suspension. Reporting is free and done through the Rent Board Portal (portal.sfrb.org). Verified

b) The allowable annual increase is set citywide, not by ZIP code. The Rent Board publishes the allowable annual increase each March 1 — currently 1.6% (March 1, 2026 – February 28, 2027) — calculated as 60% of the Bay Area CPI-U change and capped at 7% by the ordinance. A landlord may impose this increase once every 12 months on the tenant's anniversary date, without filing a petition. A "normal" increase for a continuing tenancy is therefore the allowable annual percentage (1.6% as of today), plus any properly banked prior increases, or a Board-approved petition increase (e.g., capital improvements, operating expenses). Verified

c) Larger increases require a Rent Board petition filed first. Six types of rent increases require a landlord petition filed with the Rent Board before tenants are served with notice: capital improvement passthroughs, operating & maintenance expense increases, special-circumstances increases based on comparable rents, Proposition I past-rent-history increases, utility passthroughs, and Section 1.21 tenant-in-occupancy increases. Tenants need not pay until the Board approves; if approved, the increase is retroactive to the effective date in a valid notice. There is no filing charge. Verified

d) Notice rules are strict and content-specific. Written notice 30 days before the effective date for increases of 10% or less (90 days if more than 10%, combined or alone; +5 days if mailed). The notice must state the dollar amount, percentage, effective date, and which portion is banked. Notices that do not conform can render the entire increase null and void (SF Rules & Regulations Sec. 4.10). Verified

e) State and federal law. California's AB 1482 (5% + CPI or 10% cap) does not apply to units already covered by SF rent control — SF's own rules govern. There is no federal rent increase limit or license; the federal Fair Housing Act (42 U.S.C. Sec. 3604) prohibits discriminatory terms and conditions, and California's FEHA (Gov. Code Sec. 12955) adds state protections. Verified

f) One unit or both? Raising both units using the same lawful methodology (the allowable annual percentage, on the same basis) is the lowest-risk approach and the cleanest answer to any discrimination scrutiny. Raising only one unit is not automatically unlawful, but it is harder to justify and invites review; any difference should have a documented, lawful reason (e.g., documented capital improvements in one unit).
