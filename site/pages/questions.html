Q1 — Do I still need to apply for a license and inform the Rent Board before notifying tenants, as an individual owner (not a corporation)?

Yes — for annual and banked increases. SF Admin Code Sec. 37.15 (Ordinance No. 265-20, effective January 18, 2021) requires every owner of a residential dwelling unit to report into the Rent Board Housing Inventory and, once the unit is reported as tenant-occupied, the Rent Board automatically issues a rent increase license. A landlord must have a current license on file before any annual or banked rent increase can go into effect; without it, the increase notice is not enforceable, and during any period of noncompliance the tenant is not obligated to pay the increase. The law applies to individuals and corporations alike — there is no corporate/individual distinction. For petition-based increases (capital improvements, operating expenses, etc.), a petition must be filed with the Rent Board before tenants are served with notice.

Sources: Sec. 37.15 — Reporting Obligations; Licensing · Ordinance 265-20 announcement · The Rent Board Housing Inventory · Landlord petitions

Q2 — What would be considered reasonable and normal rental increases in San Francisco (Outer Sunset, ZIP 94122) under local, state, and federal laws?

Local (SF): The normal increase is the annual allowable increase — currently 1.6% (March 1, 2026 – February 28, 2027) — once every 12 months on the anniversary date, calculated as 60% of the Bay Area CPI-U and capped at 7% by the ordinance. Plus any properly banked prior increases. This rate is set citywide (not ZIP-specific) — there is no separate 94122 rate. Anything above the allowable amount requires a Board-approved petition (capital improvements, operating expenses, etc.).

State: California's AB 1482 cap (5% + CPI or 10%) does not apply to units under SF rent control — your units are exempt from the state cap, so SF's 1.6% governs. State notice rules (Civ. Code Sec. 827) still apply: 30 days' notice for increases ≤10%, 90 days for >10%.

Federal: There is no federal rent increase limit or license. The federal Fair Housing Act (42 U.S.C. Sec. 3604) prohibits discriminatory terms and conditions — the increase must be applied on a non-discriminatory basis.

Sources: SF — Learn about rent increases · Sec. 37.3(a)(1) · SF AB 1482 page · 42 U.S.C. Sec. 3604 · Civ. Code Sec. 827

Q3 — How do big real estate corporations handle rental increases? How do individual property owners handle them in San Francisco?

Analysis (not a legal conclusion)

Same legal process for everyone. The Rent Ordinance and the Housing Inventory/license law apply to "owners of residential dwelling units" without regard to whether the owner is an individual, an LLC, a trust, or a corporation. Large corporate owners must do exactly what an individual owner must do: report into the Housing Inventory, obtain the rent increase license, file petitions for petition-based increases, and serve conforming notices. Corporate owners typically add professional property management (a manager may be designated in the Housing Inventory report for habitability issues) and volume filings (bulk reporting templates exist for 5+ units), but the obligations are identical.

Points where large owners differ:

  • Petition volume — corporate owners file capital-improvement and operating-expense petitions more often; the Rent Board adjudicates them the same way for everyone.
  • Algorithmic rent-setting is prohibited in SF — the City banned the use and sale of algorithmic devices for rent determinations (Admin Code Sec. 37.10C), which limits how even large owners may set increases.
  • Public record — Housing Inventory submissions are public (owner name, contact info, square footage, occupancy history), so corporate and individual filings are equally visible to tenants and advocates.

Sources: Sec. 37.15 · Housing Inventory (public info, bulk reporting) · Sec. 37.10C — algorithmic devices prohibited · Landlord petitions

Q4 — What is the correct way to inform tenants, and what are reasonable reasons for a rent increase?

Correct way to inform tenants: written notice, personally delivered or by mail (+5 days if mailed), 30 days before the effective date for increases ≤10% and 90 days for >10%; notice must state the dollar amount, percentage, effective date, and which portion is banked (plus any capital-improvement/utility/RAP-loan portions). Non-conforming notices can render the increase null and void (Rules & Regulations Sec. 4.10). The license must be on file with the Rent Board on the effective date.

Reasonable, lawful reasons for an increase: (a) the annual allowable increase (CPI-based, currently 1.6%); (b) banked increases from prior skipped years; (c) certified capital improvements (petition); (d) increased operating & maintenance expenses (petition); (e) utility passthroughs; (f) comparable-rent special circumstances (petition); (g) Proposition I past rent history (petition). "Market rate" alone is not a lawful reason for a continuing tenancy — that only applies to setting the initial rent on a vacant unit.

Sources: Civ. Code Sec. 827 · SF — Learn about rent increases · Rules & Regulations Sec. 4.10 · Landlord petitions and passthroughs

Q5 — SF has strict tenant protections; tenants can get free lawyers. Should the increase be able to pass review by a SF tenant lawyer?

Yes — and the way to pass that review is procedural perfection plus a lawful basis. Tenants can file Rent Board petitions (e.g., unlawful rent increase, rent history review) and obtain free counseling and legal help. A tenant lawyer reviewing a proposed increase will check: (1) is the unit covered; (2) is the anniversary date and calculation correct; (3) is there a current rent increase license; (4) was notice given with correct timing and content; (5) is the increase within the allowable amount or supported by a Board-approved petition; (6) was the same basis applied consistently (no discrimination). If all six are satisfied, the increase is defensible as normal business practice under SF's rules.

Sources: Tenant petitions · Report unlawful rent increase / rent history review · Rent Board referral listing · Rent Board counselor

Q6 — Is it OK to raise the rent on one tenant's unit, or does it make more sense to raise both units to avoid discrimination complaints?

Analysis — There is no SF law requiring identical rents across units, and no law requiring you to raise both units. Rents can lawfully differ (e.g., different lease dates, different base rents, capital improvements in one unit). However:

  • Fair Housing Act (42 U.S.C. Sec. 3604(b)) prohibits discriminating "in the terms, conditions, or privileges" of rental because of race, color, religion, sex, familial status, national origin, or handicap — and California FEHA (Gov. Code Sec. 12955) prohibits discrimination in housing terms for a broader list of protected classes.
  • Consistency is the safest practice. Raising both units on the same basis — the same allowable percentage (1.6%), same effective date, same methodology, same notice content — gives tenants and advocates a clear, non-discriminatory record and avoids any suggestion that a protected class (or a particular tenant) was singled out.
  • If only one unit is raised, the reason must be documented and lawful (e.g., documented capital improvements in that unit only, a documented rent history difference, or a Board-approved petition). Raising one unit without a documented lawful reason invites scrutiny and a tenant petition.

Recommendation: raise both units using the same lawful methodology (the allowable annual increase on each unit's anniversary date, with any properly banked amounts calculated per the same rules). This is the most defensible "normal business practice" approach.

Sources: 42 U.S.C. Sec. 3604 · Gov. Code Sec. 12955 (FEHA) · Petition requirements

Q7 — Are there any penalties for missing the license or notice requirements?

If the license is suspended or absent on the effective date, the increase notice is not enforceable and the tenant is not obligated to pay the increase for months of suspension. If notice is defective (wrong timing/content), the increase can be null and void (Rules & Regulations Sec. 4.10(b)), with a good-faith correction mechanism only for small miscalculations (≤0.5% of base rent). Tenants can file unlawful-rent-increase petitions, and AB 1482 (for non-rent-controlled units) carries civil damages up to three times the excess rent — your units are rent-controlled, so SF's remedies govern.

Sources: Sec. 37.15(e) · Rules & Regulations Sec. 4.10(b) · SF AB 1482 page (damages)
