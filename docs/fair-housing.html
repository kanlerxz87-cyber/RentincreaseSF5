Discrimination risk: one unit or both?

Whether to raise one unit or both, and what a tenant lawyer would actually look at.

Overview License Checklist Rates Units Notice Flagged Sources
Short answer to “one unit or both?” Raise both, using the same method, on each unit's own anniversary date. Not because uniformity is legally required — it isn't — but because a documented, uniformly-applied formula is the single best answer to any later claim that the difference in treatment was discriminatory.

What the law actually prohibits

Federal — Fair Housing Act. Seven protected classes: race, colour, national origin, religion, sex, familial status, disability. U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development ↗

California — FEHA. Broader. The state list includes race, colour, ancestry, national origin, citizenship, immigration status, primary language, religion, disability, sex and gender, sexual orientation, gender identity and expression, genetic information, marital status, familial status, source of income (including a Section 8 voucher), military or veteran status, and age. It also protects against retaliation, and protects people associated with someone in a protected class. State of California, Civil Rights Department ↗

Note what is not on either list: being a long-standing tenant, being on good or bad terms with the owner, or having a low rent. Raising one tenant's rent more because they have lived there longer is not, by itself, discrimination — but it is precisely the pattern a tenant lawyer will look at first.

One unit vs. both — the analysis

OptionLawful?Risk profileAssessment
Raise both, same method, each on its own anniversary Yes Lowest Recommended. The difference between Unit A (3.0%) and Unit B is fully explained by objective facts: different anniversary dates, different base rents, different banking history. That is a defence, not a liability.
Raise one unit only Yes Low to moderate Lawful if the reason is objective and documented — e.g. the other unit's anniversary has not arrived yet, or you are choosing to leave its increases banked. The risk is not the act; it is an unexplained pattern.
Raise one unit to the maximum and leave the other untouched Lawful, but Highest This is the pattern most likely to draw scrutiny. A 13–15% jump on one household while the other gets nothing invites both a discrimination complaint and a Rent Board petition.
Raise a unit shortly after the tenant complained, requested repairs, or exercised a right High risk Highest Retaliation is itself unlawful under California law, independent of whether the increase amount is correct.

State of California, Civil Rights Department ↗ U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development ↗

Why “identical” is not the right target

Unit A and Unit B cannot receive identical increases, and trying to force that would itself be arbitrary. They have different anniversary dates (February 1 and June 1), different base rents, and different banking histories. A 3.0% increase for Unit A and a different percentage for Unit B is not unequal treatment — it is the same rule applied to different facts.

What matters for defensibility is that the rule is identical:

  • same source for the percentage (the Rent Board's published rate);
  • same timing rule (the anniversary date, never earlier);
  • same treatment of banked increases (the same policy applied to both);
  • same notice period and same notice content;
  • a written record of the calculation for each unit, kept on file.

If you depart from your own rule for one household, write down the objective reason at the time. Contemporaneous notes are worth far more than a reconstruction two years later.

The recommended plan leaves Unit B's verified banked increases untouched and available for a future year. Taking them all at once is lawful but maximises both the tenant-relations cost and the legal surface area. San Francisco Rent Board ↗ San Francisco Rent Ordinance ↗

The statutory test a tenant lawyer would actually reach for Rent Ordinance §37.10B(a)(5) makes it harassment to “influence or attempt to influence a tenant to vacate a rental housing unit through fraud, intimidation or coercion” — including “by means of a rent increase that is imposed with an intent to defraud, intimidate, or coerce the tenant into vacating.” The ordinance then spells out what counts as evidence of bad faith: “(1) the rent increase was substantially in excess of market rates for comparable units; (2) the rent increase was within six months after an attempt to recover possession of the unit; and (3) such other factors as a court or the Rent Board may deem relevant.” San Francisco Rent Ordinance ↗

Remedies are not symbolic: treble actual damages or $1,000 in statutory damages — whichever is greater — plus reasonable attorney's fees and costs to a prevailing plaintiff. San Francisco Rent Ordinance ↗

Measured against that test, a 3.0% anniversary increase on Unit A is nowhere near “substantially in excess of market rates,” and neither unit's increase follows an attempt to recover possession. That is the difference between an increase that survives scrutiny and one that does not.
One exposure worth naming Unit A's tenancy changed composition during the term. If any increase, deposit change or fee coincided with, or followed, a request from an occupant relating to a disability, a reasonable accommodation, or a protected characteristic, the timing alone can carry a retaliation claim regardless of the amount. California law protects against retaliation for exercising fair housing rights. State of California, Civil Rights Department ↗
