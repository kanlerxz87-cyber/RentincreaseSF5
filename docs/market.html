What "normal" looks like in the Outer Sunset, and what the rate history is

Two different questions hide inside "what is a normal increase": what the law allows (a hard number), and what comparable units actually rent for (a distribution). The first is exact; the second is only as good as the City's self-reported inventory.

2. Why the state cap does not raise the ceiling

AB 1482 caps rent increases at 5% plus CPI or 10%, whichever is lower, for covered units - but §1947.12(d)(3) excludes housing subject to a local ordinance that restricts annual increases below that level. San Francisco's 1.6% is far below it, so the local cap governs and the AB 1482 cap never binds for a covered unit here. S11

Federal law adds no ceiling: the Fair Housing Act governs who you may discriminate against, not how much you may charge. S14

4. Reading this honestly

  • These are contract rents already in force, not asking rents. They lag the market by construction.
  • Bands are $250 wide and self-reported, so a "median band" is the band containing the median, not a median dollar figure.
  • Neither unit's bedroom count was supplied, so all three distributions are shown (FL-06). At $3,995.00, Unit 1 sits near the top of the one-bedroom range, mid-range for two-bedrooms and below the middle of three-bedrooms. At $3,150.00, Unit 2 is mid-to-upper for one-bedrooms, near the middle for two-bedrooms and below the middle for three-bedrooms.
  • The inventory is not a rent-cap dataset and has no notion of "normal increase". The only official definition of a normal increase is the published percentage.

Why this data matters legally. §37.10B(a)(5) treats a rent increase "substantially in excess of market rates for comparable units" as evidence of bad faith where a tenant believes an increase is aimed at getting them out. Because this inventory is public, a tenant advocate can run the same comparison in minutes. Knowing where your rent sits in the distribution is therefore not market research - it is risk assessment.
