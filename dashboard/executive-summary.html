Executive summary

The Rent Board question first, then what it means for these two units

You read that you must inform the Rent Board and apply for a licence to stop a rent increase from being challenged. The requirement is real. The mechanism is not what you read.

There is no application, no approval step and no letter to the Rent Board before you notify tenants. What the law requires is that each unit be reported in the Rent Board's Housing Inventory, which is an annual online submission due March 1 and is free. Once a tenant-occupied unit is reported, a rent increase licence is generated automatically. That licence must be in effect on the date the increase takes effect - not merely by the time you write the notice. If it is not, the licence is treated as suspended for the period of noncompliance and the tenant is not obligated to pay the increased rent for those months; the Rent Board's own page adds that a notice is not enforceable without a licence on its effective date.

And being an individual owner rather than a corporation changes nothing. The ordinance applies to "owners of residential dwelling units subject to this Chapter 37" without any size, entity or corporate threshold, and the licence provision is written the same way.

Sequence therefore: report the unit → confirm the licence shows in the Rent Board Portal → calculate at the published rate → serve a conforming notice in time. An increase with perfect arithmetic served out of that order is still void, because a nonconforming increase is null and void under §37.3(b)(5) and a suspended licence defeats payment under §37.15(e).

1. What the law actually allows, in numbers

For an effective date between March 1, 2026 and February 28, 2027 the allowable annual increase is 1.6% of base rent (Rent Board Form 571; Rent Board rent-increase guidance). Three limits sit around that number:

  • One increase per 12 months, on the tenancy's anniversary date, with the first available 12 months after the lease began.
  • No compounding and no rounding up. Multiple skipped years are added as percentages, then applied once to base rent.
  • The 7% ceiling in the ordinance means the annual figure can never exceed 7% even if CPI spikes; this year it is 1.6% because the formula is 60% of the Bay Area CPI change.

Anything above the cap requires a Rent Board petition filed before the notice: capital improvement, operating and maintenance, comparable rents, Prop I rent history, utility passthroughs, or a §1.21 increase. Filing is free and tenants do not pay until approval.

3. The reason there is a favourite and a risky option

Unit 2 has not had an increase since 2019, so six years of published percentages have accumulated: 1.8 + 0.7 + 2.3 + 3.6 + 1.7 + 1.4 = 11.5%, plus the current 1.6% = 13.1%. That is lawful and needs no petition. It is also the single number in this file most likely to produce a tenant petition, because a 13.1% single-step increase is the kind of fact pattern the ordinance's harassment section points at: §37.10B(a)(5) lists a rent increase "substantially in excess of market rates for comparable units" as evidence of bad faith where the tenant believes the increase is aimed at getting them to leave. There is no suggestion here that it is - but the notice has to survive a stranger reading it that way.

4. A conflict you should know about before you rely on any number

FL-03. The Rent Board's "Banked rent increases" page says a skipped increase is banked only if 24 full months pass without any rent increase. The ordinance itself, §37.3(a)(2), contains no 24-month condition - it allows a skipped increase to be imposed on "the tenant's subsequent rent increase anniversary dates" - and the Rent Board's own worked examples bank increases after 12 months. This is the difference between Unit 1 taking $63.92 or $119.85 on February 1, 2027. We did not resolve it in your favour or against you: both options are priced, the safe one is labelled safe, and a written answer from a Rent Board counsellor is the next step.

5. Discrimination: one unit or both?

No reviewed source - federal, state or local - requires you to increase both units or to increase them equally. Every rule constrains the amount, the timing, the notice and the licensing. What is prohibited is different treatment because of a protected characteristic: race, colour, religion, sex, gender identity or expression, sexual orientation, marital status, national origin, ancestry, familial status, source of income, disability, veteran status or genetic information under FEHA §12955, and race, colour, national origin, religion, sex, familial status or disability under the federal Fair Housing Act. Violating those laws is separately bad-faith harassment under §37.10B(a)(9), which carries treble damages and attorney fees.

Raising both to look even is the weaker strategy here, because the two tenancies are not in the same position: Unit 1 is 21 months old with a skipped 2026 anniversary, Unit 2 has six banked years. Forcing one timetable would either impose a catch-up on Unit 1 it is not entitled to, or forfeit Unit 2's accumulated entitlement. The defensible position is the opposite of uniformity: treat each tenancy on its own anniversary, and keep a one-page file note per unit recording the anniversary date, the published rate, the licence check and the service date.

6. Would a San Francisco tenant lawyer be able to overturn these increases?

Not on the law, if four objective things are true: the units are covered; the licence was in effect on the effective date; the percentage is at or under the published rate; and the notice conforms to §37.3(b) and was served at least 30 days ahead (90 days above 10%). A tenant's remedy is a Rent Board petition - free, no time limit, heard by an Administrative Law Judge - and the Board can void only increases that break those rules. Free legal representation under the Tenant Right to Counsel programme attaches to eviction notices and unlawful detainer lawsuits, not to a rent-increase notice on its own; the petition process is expressly built for unrepresented parties.

There is also one challenge that has nothing to do with the percentage: a tenant who has asked for repairs required by law and not received them can petition on Form 516B to defer the annual increase, filing within 60 days of receiving the notice. If granted, the increase is postponed until the work is done and any increase already collected is refunded. A textbook-conforming 1.6% notice is still deferrable that way, which is why the repair file matters as much as the arithmetic.

What genuinely draws fire is not arithmetic but a catch-up that looks like pressure, an increase served on a tenant who has just complained about repairs, or a difference between two apparently identical tenancies that nobody wrote down. Those are the risks this file manages - not the 1.6%.
