Unit 1 — continuing tenancy ($3,995; last lease 2025-02-01; continuing from 2026-04-01)

Because initial tenant U1-A remained when U1-B/U1-C vacated and U1-D moved in, this is a continuing tenancy. The 2025-02-01 lease therefore could not be a “new tenancy” with an unrestricted initial rent (that carve-out requires no prior tenant to remain — Civ. Code §1947.12(b); F-03). The pre-2025 rent is not documented (F-01), so two scenarios:

Scenario 1 — 2025-02-01 set/increased rent to $3,995 (anniversary = 2025-02-01)
Effective 2026-02-01 → 02-281.4% → +$55.93 → $4,050.93 (30-day notice)
Effective 2026-03-01 or later1.6% + 1.4% banked = 3.0% → +$119.85 → $4,114.85 (30-day notice)
CheckThe 2025-02-01 increase itself must have been licensed/noticed/within cap then (1.7% period + banked) — F-11
Scenario 2 — rent already $3,995 before 2025-02-01 (anniversary = last actual increase, unknown)
Banked total= sum of rates for each skipped year since the last actual increase (needs ledger)
Upper-bound bank (no increase since before 2019-03-01)14.1% banked + 1.6% = 15.7% → +$627.22 → $4,622.22 (90-day notice, >10%)
Actual figurecannot be computed without the rent ledger — F-01

Both scenarios: base rent only, no rounding up, no charge for Tenant U1-D’s occupancy (sf.gov “Additional occupants”), license current on the effective date, 30/90-day written notice. Full derivation: data/analysis.md.

“Reasonable and normal” — answer The only defensible benchmark is the lawful maximum: 1.6% (2026-27) plus documented banked increases, licensed, on time, with proper notice. Any increase at or below that, applied on consistent, documented criteria to both units, is within the range a tenant-side review would treat as normal business practice. Market-rate numbers are deliberately excluded from this site’s tables (non-official source).
