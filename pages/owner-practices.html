Owner playbook

How institutions and individual owners handle increases, whether to raise one unit or both, what counts as a lawful reason, and the policy that makes the answer defensible.

“How do big real estate corporations handle increases?”

The honest answer is that they are subject to exactly the same cap, and the differences are procedural rather than legal. Nothing in the Rent Ordinance scales the allowance to the size of the owner, and nothing exempts an individual owner from the reporting duties. What differs is administration — and in one respect the law treats corporate owners worse.

Practical takeaway: borrow the institutional process — an annual review on each anniversary date, a notice built from the checklist, a rent ledger and a dated licence record — while using the latitude an individual owner has: the law does not require you to take the maximum, and a documented decision not to take it is itself evidence of reasonableness.

“Should I raise one unit or both?”

The law does not require equal treatment, and the arithmetic will never produce it. Each tenancy has its own anniversary date, base rent and bank, so the maximum lawful increase differs by construction: 3.0% for Unit A and 13.1% for Unit B. Chapter 37 and the Rules were reviewed for a same-increase-across-units requirement and none was found; that absence is recorded as a finding, not as permission to be arbitrary.

Can a rent increase itself be unlawful discrimination?Yes, if it is imposed because of a protected characteristic, or if the terms differ on that basis.S25 S26
Does a difference in percentage prove discrimination?No. Different base rents, tenancy lengths and banks produce lawful differences in percentage. What matters is the reason and the pattern.S25
Is the ordinance's harassment section relevant?Yes: a rent increase used to influence a tenant to vacate, or one that violates anti-discrimination law, is harassment under §37.10B.S21
What makes the pattern defensible?One written method applied to both units, documented reasons tied to the tenancy, and no correlation between the size of the increase and any protected characteristic.S25 S21
Recommendation. Raise both units, using the same documented method — but expect the percentages to diverge, because the ledgers diverge, and say so in each notice. Raising only one unit is lawful where the reason is objective and documented, for example that the other tenancy's anniversary date has not arrived; but with two units under one roof a selective increase invites the question, and Unit B's 13.1% is already the largest single fact in this file. The cleanest sequence is Unit A's 3.0% on 2027-02-01, then Unit B's 13.1% on its own anniversary, each with its own notice and ledger. Whether to run both in the same cycle is a decision for the owner with counsel; the legal record supports either, provided the method is uniform and documented.

A one-page rent-increase policy you can point to

  1. Scope. Applies to every residential tenancy in the building, without exception.
  2. Timing. Reviewed once a year on each tenancy's anniversary date; never more than once in 12 months S1.
  3. Amount. The published annual allowance plus any properly banked allowance; never above the allowance without a Rent Board petition S5.
  4. Calculation. Percentages added, not compounded, not rounded up; the arithmetic is attached to the notice S9.
  5. Licence gate. No notice is served unless the Housing Inventory report is filed and the licence is current S3 S4.
  6. Notice. 30 or 90 days (plus five if mailed), with the dollar amount, percentage, effective date and annual/banked composition S16 S5.
  7. Records. Notice, proof of service, rent ledger, licence record and calculation sheet kept for the life of the tenancy S20.
  8. Fairness. No increase is timed to a repair request, a complaint, a protected-characteristic event, or anything that resembles pressure to vacate S21 S25.
  9. Review. Any deviation is approved in writing before service, with the reason recorded.
