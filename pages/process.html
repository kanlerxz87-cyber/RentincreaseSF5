The Rent Board Process — Step by Step

What an individual owner must do before, during, and after a rent increase · Every step linked to an official source
Bottom line: You are an individual owner, not a corporation. That does not exempt you from the Rent Board process. Under SF Admin Code Sec. 37.15, all owners of residential units must report into the Housing Inventory and hold a rent increase license to impose annual or banked increases — and petitions are required before notice for other increase types. Sec. 37.15 text · Ordinance 265-20

Steps to a lawful rent increase

Confirm your units are covered by the SF Rent Ordinance

Rent control and eviction protections apply to residential units built on or before June 13, 1979 (i.e., buildings with a certificate of final occupancy before that date). Your building was built in 1940 per the owner, so the units are covered. You can verify with the SF Planning Property Information Map or by calling a Rent Board counselor.

Source: SF rental laws — learn about San Francisco rental laws

Pay the annual Rent Board Fee (due March 1)

Every owner of a residential dwelling unit must pay the Rent Board Fee each year unless exempt. For 2026–2027 the fee is $59.00 per dwelling unit, due March 1 (late penalties of 5%/month, capped at 15%). After paying in full, the owner may recover 50% ($29.50) from each tenant who lived in the unit as of November 1. Pay through the Rent Board Portal. Note: This fee is separate from the rent increase license.

Source: The Rent Board Fee · Admin Code Chapter 37A (fee)

Report into the Rent Board Housing Inventory → receive the rent increase license

This is the "inform the Rent Board / get the license" step. Owners report unit information online through the Rent Board Portal (portal.sfrb.org) — free, 24/7, under penalty of perjury. Reported information includes owner contact details, square footage, bedrooms/bathrooms, occupancy status and date, and base rent (in $250 increments).

Deadline: March 1 each year (for buildings with fewer than 10 units, reporting began March 1, 2023). Once you report a unit as tenant-occupied, the Rent Board automatically generates a rent increase license. A landlord must have a current license on file before any annual or banked increase goes into effect — and the notice is not enforceable without it. The license does not need to be included with the rent increase notice.

Sources: Sec. 37.15 — Reporting Obligations; Licensing · Ordinance 265-20 announcement · The Rent Board Housing Inventory

Choose the type of increase

Three lawful routes:

(a) Annual allowable increase — once every 12 months, on the tenant's anniversary date, without filing a petition. Current rate: 1.6% (3/1/2026–2/28/2027). Requires only the license (Step 3) and proper notice (Step 6).

(b) Banked increases — increases skipped in prior years may be banked (only if 24+ months pass without any increase; no limit on amount; does not expire; does not carry over to a new tenant). Requires only the license and notice.

(c) Petition-based increases — for increases above the annual allowable amount, the landlord must file a petition with the Rent Board before serving tenants with notice. The six petition types: capital improvements; operating & maintenance expenses; special circumstances (comparable rents); Proposition I past rent history; utility passthroughs; and Section 1.21 tenant-in-occupancy. Tenants do not pay until the Board approves; if approved, the increase is retroactive to the effective date of a valid notice. Petition filing is free (an independent estimator fee may apply in some capital improvement cases).

Sources: Learn about rent increases · Banked rent increases · Landlord petitions and passthroughs

Calculate the amount correctly

Multiply the tenant's base rent (including housing services like parking/storage, excluding temporary passthroughs) by the applicable percentage. Rent increases cannot be rounded up to the nearest dollar. Banked amounts are added to the current annual percentage and applied to base rent (not compounded or prorated).

Source: Learn about rent increases — calculation examples · Banked rent increases — calculation

Serve proper written notice

Deliver a written notice: 30 days before the effective date for increases of 10% or less (alone or combined), or 90 days if the increase exceeds 10%. +5 days if mailed. The notice must state the dollar amount, the percentage, the effective date, and which portion (if any) is banked. Use the percentage in effect on the effective date. Non-conforming notices can render the increase null and void (SF Rules & Regulations Sec. 4.10).

Sources: CA Civil Code Sec. 827(b) · SF — Learn about rent increases (notice) · Rules & Regulations Sec. 4.10 (notice content)

Keep records and stay compliant

Keep the license current, update the Housing Inventory within 30 days of any change to owner contact information, keep a complete rent history, and keep proof of notice. Tenants may challenge an increase at the Rent Board (tenant petitions — e.g., unlawful rent increase, decrease in housing services). A complete, correct file is the best defense. You can also request a Rent Board determination of the tenant's current lawful rent.

Sources: Housing Inventory (30-day updates) · Tenant petitions · Report unlawful rent increase / rent history review

Good news for the owner: none of this is only for corporations. The Housing Inventory law and the license requirement apply to "owners of residential dwelling units" without distinction — individuals, LLCs, trusts, and corporations all follow the same process. The Rent Board also provides free counseling for both landlords and tenants: (415) 252-4600, weekdays 9:00–12:00 and 1:00–4:00.
