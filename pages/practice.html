Practice: Corporate vs. Individual Owners

How increases are actually handled, how to notify tenants, and what “reasonable reasons” mean. Industry-practice items are labeled as non-legal; legal items carry official sources.

How big operators handle rent increases industry practice — non-legal

  • Annual cycle tied to anniversaries/renewals. Increases are scheduled around each tenancy’s anniversary date or lease renewal, not ad hoc. (NAA — How to Increase Rent Without Losing Tenants)
  • Advance communication well beyond the legal minimum. Large teams commonly start the renewal conversation 60–90 days ahead of the legal 30-day notice window; one industry study reports advance notice reduces move-outs materially. (NAA; secondary industry summary citing NAA data secondary)
  • Documented justification. Increases are supported by market-comparable rent data and cost documentation (taxes, insurance, maintenance, CPI) so the amount is defensible in a review. (NAA)
  • Gradual steps and retention tools. Phased increases (commonly ~3–5%/yr outside rent control), renewal incentives, and loyalty concessions rather than one large jump. (NAA; Apartments.com landlord guide)
  • Centralized compliance. Property management functions track Housing Inventory filings (by 3/1), Rent Board fees, license status, notice calendars, and proof of service in one system — this is the institutional version of the documentation file recommended on the Process page. (SF-specific compliance reporting: sf.gov Housing Inventory)

In San Francisco, corporations are subject to the identical legal requirements as individual owners (license, fee, cap, notice) — the difference is operational scale and documentation discipline, not legal treatment. (sf.gov: all owners must report.)

How individual owners in SF handle it

  • Same legal channel, smaller scale: one Portal account, two units — the annual March 1 inventory report, the $59/unit fee, license confirmation, and the notice itself. (sf.gov: Housing Inventory, Rent Board Fee)
  • Free counseling is available to landlords too. The Rent Board provides counseling by phone and in person to tenants and landlords (311 / 415-701-2311). Using it before serving notices is low-cost de-risking. (About Rent Board)
  • The practical gap is the rent ledger. Individual owners most often lack the contemporaneous record of which years were skipped (banked), which is exactly what a review will test — see Unit 1 flag F-01.
  • Keep the same habits as corporate teams: fixed annual review date per unit (the anniversary), written notice with proof of service, and a per-unit compliance file.

Best-practice extras (non-legal): state the reason plainly (see below), thank the tenant, give contact info, and — where the increase is sizable — consider offering a call or a short explanation in advance of the formal notice (NAA). None of this is required by law; it is what makes an increase read as “normal business practice” in a review.

Reasonable reasons for a rent increase

  • No statutory “reason” is required for a lawful increase that stays within the cap: the official procedure (license → compute on base rent → anniversary timing → written notice) contains no justification step. (sf.gov learn-about page)
  • Legitimate business reasons owners commonly give (industry practice, not legal requirements): rising operating costs, property tax and insurance, inflation (the cap itself is CPI-linked at 60%), building maintenance, and market conditions. (NAA — source industry)
  • What must never be the reason: the tenant’s protected class (race, color, religion, sex, familial status, national origin, disability — 42 U.S.C. §3604), retaliation for exercising rights (§37.9(d) — official text; 42 U.S.C. §3617 — CRS), or a pretext to force a vacancy (§37.9F — text).
  • For your building, the cleanest reason statement is the arithmetic itself: “the city’s allowable annual increase for 2026-03-01→2027-02-28 is 1.6% (SF Rent Board), plus documented banked increases from years in which no increase was taken.” That is factual, verifiable by the tenant in the public announcements, and hard to characterize as anything other than normal.
Sourcing note There is no “official” source for how corporations handle increases (it is business practice, not law); the items above are therefore labeled industry practice and cite national industry sources (NAA) or clearly-labeled secondary summaries.
