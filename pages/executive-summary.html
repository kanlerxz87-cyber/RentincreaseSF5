Executive summary

Two rent-controlled units, one owner, and one question that had to be answered before anything else: does an individual owner need a Rent Board licence before raising rent? Then the numbers, the risks and the decisions.

1. The Rent Board question — answered first

Short answer: as an individual owner you are not exempt, and you are not applying for permission. Two separate legal duties are being mixed together in the advice you read.

(a) There is no application and no approval for a normal increase. The Rent Ordinance says that a landlord who has reported the required information to the Rent Board “shall have a licence to impose annually a rent increase which does not exceed a tenant's base rent by more than 60% of said published increase”, capped at 7% S5. The Rent Board's own rate sheet says the annual increase may be imposed “without filing a petition at the Rent Board” S2, and its Rules are titled “Rent Increases Not Requiring Approval by the Rent Board” S9.

(b) There is a licence, and it is automatic but conditional. Every owner must report into the Housing Inventory; a tenant-occupied unit then receives a rent increase licence automatically, and the licence must be current on the effective date of the notice or the increase is not enforceable S3 S4. The statute adds that during a suspension “a tenant shall not be obligated to pay the increased rent” S4.

(c) Ownership structure is irrelevant to both duties. Nothing in Chapter 37 exempts a natural person who owns a duplex: the reporting duty is on “owners of residential dwelling units” S4 and the fee ordinance applies to “the owner of each residential unit” S8. Being an individual rather than a corporation changes nothing here.

(d) What would have required a petition: only capital improvement, operating & maintenance, comparable-rents, Prop I rent history, utility passthrough and §1.21 increases, all filed before notice, and none payable by the tenant until approved S19.

Practical translation: file the inventory report (free, online, due March 1), confirm the licence is live before you serve the notice, then serve a compliant notice. No cover letter to the Rent Board and no approval letter is required. Full sequence: Rent Board process.

4. Is this “reasonable and normal”? What the standard actually is

“Reasonable and normal” has a precise meaning in San Francisco, and it is not a market comparison: the allowance the Rent Board sets each year is the normal increase. An owner who imposes the annual allowance, or properly banked accumulated allowances, is inside the safe harbour by definition and needs no justification S5 S2. Anything above it is not normal until a Rent Board Administrative Law Judge says it is, and the landlord carries the burden of proof S5 S30.

5. Would this survive a tenant-side lawyer review?

A tenant can file a petition alleging an unlawful rent increase at any time, free of charge, with no filing deadline S20. The review that follows is narrow and predictable, and it turns on documents rather than intentions. Six things decide it:

  1. Was the licence live? Inventory report filed, licence current on the effective date S3 S4.
  2. Is the arithmetic right? Correct percentage for each year, added not compounded, not rounded up S2 S9.
  3. Was the notice long enough and complete? 30/90 days (plus 5 if mailed), dollar amount, percentage, effective date, annual vs banked split S16 S5 S11.
  4. Was the anniversary respected? No increase within 12 months S1.
  5. Is the pattern neutral? The same method applied to both units, in writing, with no protected characteristic correlating with the difference S25 S21.
  6. Could it read as pressure to leave? §37.10B(a)(5) makes a rent increase evidence of bad faith when it is used to influence a tenant to vacate S21.

The full fifteen-question version, with the evidence to keep for each answer, is on Tenant-lawyer test. The policy side, including the one-unit-or-both question, is on Owner playbook.
